# Coin: Programmable Card Made Obsolete by EMV Chip Mandate

## Core Failed Assumption
**"Hardware timing risk can be managed when the product cycle is 12+ months."**

---

## Case Overview
- **Company**: Coin
- **Founded**: 2012
- **Shut Down**: 2017
- **Total Funding**: ~$15 million
- **Founders**: Kanishk Parashar
- **Category**: competition

---

## What They Believed (Hypothesis)
Coin believed that its programmable magnetic-stripe card — which stored up to eight payment cards — would reach market before card network EMV chip mandates made magnetic-stripe-only devices obsolete, and before mobile wallets gained mainstream adoption.

**Specific Claims:**
- Consumers want a single card to replace their entire wallet
- Magnetic stripe remains viable for the lifetime of the first product generation
- Mobile payment adoption will remain slow enough for hardware wallets to establish a market

---

## What Actually Happened (Reality)
EMV chip card adoption was mandated by Visa and Mastercard for US merchants with a liability shift in October 2015. Coin's device used only magnetic stripe and could not store or transmit EMV chip data. Simultaneously, Apple Pay launched in 2014 and Android Pay in 2015. Coin was acquired by Fitbit in 2016 and shut down in 2017 without delivering a viable product.

**Key Metrics:**
- **EMV Mandate**: October 2015 US liability shift
- **Apple Pay Launch**: October 2014
- **Coin Acquisition**: Fitbit 2016, product shut down 2017
- **Pre-Orders**: ~$15M raised / significant pre-order volume that went unfulfilled at scale

---

## Why It Failed

### Primary Reason: Two Simultaneous Platform Shifts Obsoleted the Hardware Before It Scaled
EMV chip adoption and mobile wallets each independently would have damaged the business; both together were fatal.

**Evidence:**
- Magnetic-stripe-only card cannot be used at chip-required terminals
- Apple Pay and Android Pay eliminated the card consolidation problem through software
- Hardware product cycles of 12-18 months left no room to pivot once mandates were announced
- Card network EMV timelines were publicly known and incorporated into Coin's product roadmap — they misjudged speed of merchant compliance

---

## Lessons for Startup Critique

### For Similar Assumptions
> "Hardware products in regulated infrastructure categories carry timing risk that software cannot hedge."

**Red Flags:**
- Product depends on the continued dominance of a specific technical standard (magnetic stripe)
- Platform incumbents (Apple, Google) have clear strategic interest in the same category
- Regulatory timelines for infrastructure transitions are publicly known during development

### Counterargument Template
> "Coin illustrates that hardware startups in payment infrastructure face compounding timing risk that a single product cycle cannot absorb. The EMV mandate and mobile wallet launches were both foreseeable during development — the error was underestimating the speed of merchant compliance and consumer mobile wallet adoption. Any hardware product whose value proposition is neutralized by a regulatory transition or a platform feature launch should be required to demonstrate a pivot path before the transition occurs."
