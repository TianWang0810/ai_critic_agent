# Kiko: Web Calendar Killed by Google Calendar Launch

## Core Failed Assumption
**"A niche productivity category is defensible against a platform that can add the feature for free."**

---

## Case Overview
- **Company**: Kiko
- **Founded**: 2005
- **Shut Down**: 2006
- **Total Funding**: ~$70,000 (YC W2005 batch)
- **Founders**: Justin Kan, Emmett Shear
- **Category**: competition

---

## What They Believed (Hypothesis)
Kiko believed that a well-designed web-based calendar could sustain a subscription business in a market that lacked a dominant free option, and that user experience differentiation would create retention even after competitors entered.

**Specific Claims:**
- Paid web calendar fills a genuine market gap for users who want browser-based scheduling
- First-mover advantage in web calendars creates switching costs
- Design quality is a defensible differentiator in productivity software

---

## What Actually Happened (Reality)
Google launched Google Calendar in April 2006, free for all Gmail users. Kiko's subscription model became untenable overnight. The founders sold the entire company on eBay for $258,100, then went on to build Twitch.

**Key Metrics:**
- **Google Calendar Launch**: April 2006
- **Kiko eBay Sale**: August 2006 for $258,100
- **Funding**: ~$70K from Y Combinator
- **Founder Outcome**: Justin Kan and Emmett Shear founded Justin.tv / Twitch

---

## Why It Failed

### Primary Reason: Platform Entrant Made the Category Structurally Unprofitable Overnight
Google's distribution and zero marginal cost made any subscription calendar unviable.

**Evidence:**
- Google Calendar launched free with Gmail integration reaching tens of millions of users
- No feature advantage could justify a paid subscription against a free platform product
- Switching cost in calendar software is low — contacts and events are easily exported
- Kiko had no platform relationship or integration leverage to compete with Google's ecosystem

---

## Lessons for Startup Critique

### For Similar Assumptions
> "Productivity tools that platforms can replicate for free have no durable subscription business."

**Red Flags:**
- Product category is a feature, not a standalone market
- Revenue model depends on users paying for something a platform incumbent could offer at zero marginal cost
- No proprietary data or network effect that a platform cannot replicate with existing user relationships

### Counterargument Template
> "Kiko is the foundational example of platform-kill risk in productivity software. When a product is a feature rather than a standalone market, a platform entrant with distribution and zero marginal cost can make the entire category economically unviable in a single launch. The relevant question for any productivity startup is not 'can we build this better?' but 'what prevents Google, Microsoft, or Apple from shipping this as a free addition to a product their users already depend on?' Without a clear answer, the business model is structurally fragile."
